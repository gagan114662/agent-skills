# Marketing automation with approval gates

There are two ways to automate marketing. The first removes the human entirely and hopes nothing
embarrassing goes out. The second removes the human from the *drudgery* and keeps them firmly on the
*decisions*. This guide is about the second one — because it's the only kind we'd trust with our own
brand.

## The failure mode of "full" automation

Hand a tool the keys to your social account and tell it to "grow engagement," and one of two things
happens: it does nothing useful, or it does something you'd never have approved. The problem isn't the
automation — it's the missing gate between *drafting* and *doing*.

A tweet that's wrong is a Tuesday. A tweet that's wrong, sent to 50,000 followers, unreviewed, is a
crisis. The cost of a mistake scales with reach, and reach is exactly what automation adds.

## The pattern: draft freely, gate the send

The fix is an old idea from software — separate the thing that *proposes* a change from the thing that
*applies* it. In marketing terms:

1. **Agents draft freely.** Researching, writing, planning, auditing — none of that touches the outside
   world. Let it run all day.
2. **Anything that leaves the building stops at a gate.** A social post, an email blast, ad spend, a
   published page — each becomes a request a human reviews.
3. **A human approves the good ones.** One click. The draft goes out exactly as written, or with an
   edit, or not at all.

The agents never had the authority to send in the first place. The gate isn't a safety net bolted on
afterward — it's the default.

## What belongs behind a gate

- **Outbound sends:** posts, emails, DMs, anything published to an audience.
- **Money:** ad spend, paid promotions. Gate these *and* put a spend threshold on them.
- **Irreversible actions:** anything you can't quietly undo.

What *doesn't* need a gate: research, drafts, internal plans, analytics. Automate those without
hesitation — that's where the hours actually go.

## Why "sensitive by default" matters

The safest default is the one where a *new* kind of outbound action is gated until you say otherwise —
not the other way around. If someone adds a new send channel next quarter, you want it gated on day one,
not silently auto-approved because nobody remembered to add a rule. Default to caution; opt in to
automation deliberately.

## Make the gate fast, not bureaucratic

A gate only works if approving is *easy*. The review should show you exactly what would go out, in one
line, with one click to approve. If reviewing is painful, people rubber-stamp — and a rubber-stamped
gate is no gate at all. Keep the queue short, the summaries honest, and the approve button close.

## The one-line version

Automate the work. Gate the judgement. Let the agents draft everything and decide nothing that reaches
the world — that's the whole discipline, and it's how you get the speed of automation without the
3am-deleting-a-tweet part.

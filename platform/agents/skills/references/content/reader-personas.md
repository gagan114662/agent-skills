# Reader Personas

Writing for "everyone" produces copy that moves no one. Every word is calibrated for **one reader** at **one stage**. The most common reason a competent draft fails: it addresses an audience that doesn't exist — an average of three real readers, sharp for none of them.

## Identify the one reader

Before drafting, write one sentence: **"This is for [specific person] who is trying to [specific job] but is blocked by [specific obstacle]."**

- Not "marketers" → "a solo founder doing their own SEO who just got a Search Console warning they don't understand."
- Not "developers" → "a backend engineer who knows SQL cold but has never touched a vector database and is under deadline."

Specificity is counterintuitively *more* reach, not less: the named reader feels seen, and adjacent readers self-select in. Generic copy makes nobody feel seen.

## Match the awareness stage (Eugene Schwartz's five, collapsed to three)

The reader's stage dictates message, depth, and CTA. Misjudging it is fatal: pitch the product to a problem-aware reader and you lose them; over-explain the problem to a product-aware reader and you bore them.

**Problem-aware** — feels the pain, doesn't know solutions exist.
- *Message:* name and dramatize the problem; make them feel understood. Agitate before you teach.
- *Depth:* high on the problem, low on your product (don't pitch yet).
- *CTA:* soft — "learn what's actually causing this." Subscribe, read more. Asking for the sale here repels.

**Solution-aware** — knows solution categories exist, comparing approaches.
- *Message:* this *type* of solution beats the alternatives; here's how to evaluate.
- *Depth:* mechanism, trade-offs, comparison. They want to decide between approaches.
- *CTA:* medium — "see how X approaches this," a demo, a guide.

**Product-aware** — knows your product, weighing whether to commit.
- *Message:* why you specifically — proof, differentiation, objection-handling, price/risk reversal.
- *Depth:* case studies, specifics, the parts they'll ask about anyway.
- *CTA:* direct and strong — "start free," "book a call," "buy."

One piece serves one stage. A "what is X" explainer is problem/solution-aware; a case study is product-aware. Don't try to span all three in one article — you'll dilute each.

## The before & after state

Anchor the piece on a transformation, not features. Make both states concrete:

- **Before:** what the reader has/feels/does now (the painful status quo). "You export to CSV, hand-fix 200 rows, re-import, and pray."
- **After:** the world once the job is done. "Rows map automatically; bad data fails loudly before it ships."

Readers buy the *gap* between before and after — the size of that delta is your persuasion. Features are just the bridge; lead with the far bank.

## Jobs-to-be-done framing

People don't want your content or your product; they "hire" both to make progress on a job. Frame around the job:

- **Functional job:** the practical task — "validate a CSV before importing."
- **Emotional job:** how they want to feel — "confident the import won't silently corrupt data."
- **Social job:** how they want to be seen — "the person on the team who doesn't ship broken data."

Write to all three when you can. The functional job gets them to click; the emotional and social jobs get them to act. A headline that names the job ("Stop hand-fixing CSV imports before every upload") outperforms one that names a feature.

## Why "everyone" fails — and the test

Averaging readers produces tepid copy: too basic for experts, too advanced for novices, urgent for no one. The fix is the **read-back test**: hand the draft to someone who *isn't* your target reader. If they say "this is clearly for [your exact target], not me" — perfect. If they say "this could be for anyone" — you've failed; sharpen until it excludes the wrong reader on purpose.
